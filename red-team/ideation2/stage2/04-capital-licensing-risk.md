# Capital, licensing and liability: stage-2 evaluation

Lens 04, round 2, stage 2. 4 Nov 2026. Australian English, AUD. 18 web searches. www.wa.gov.au could not be reached from this session, so WA duty points rest on search extracts. Labels: **[verified: source]**, **[estimate]**, **[unverified]**. This lens doesn't give legal advice: every licensing conclusion below is a working view to put to a regulatory lawyer for about A$15–30k before the founder quits.

## Assumptions

These apply to every candidate.

- **Stay-employed baseline (B0):** A$3.25M of 10-year value (midpoint of the A$3.0–3.5M used in rounds 1 and 2), pre-tax and nominal, with no capital at risk.
- **Founder salary after quitting:** nil in the first 12 months, A$150k in years 2–3, A$200k from year 4. That is about A$1.5M pre-tax over 10 years. "Positive" means salary plus equity at year 10 exceeds A$3.25M, so equity must reach about **A$1.8M**.
- **Tax:** equity is held personally or through a family trust for more than 12 months, so the 50% CGT discount applies. Salary is taxed at about 45%. This favours equity outcomes by about 15–20% against B0 after tax. I don't adjust the scores for it, but it is a further reason to keep salary low. **Non-cash fees (royalties, warrants) are taxed as income when received** [estimate: general principle for non-cash consideration; confirm], so they create a tax bill with no cash to pay it.
- **Time discount:** 8% a year. A A$10M equity value in year 10 is worth about A$4.6M today. This hurts every candidate whose value arrives late (S7, S8, VoltCover).
- **Dilution:** services businesses give 15–25% to a co-founder and 10% to an ESOP, with no institutional round. Balance-sheet businesses dilute the founder to 20–45% of the operating company, plus a promote or carry.
- **Staging:** every candidate is tested for 6–18 months while employed. Where employer consent is needed, I assume it is given. Founder capital at risk is capped at **A$1.2M**, and the founder **gives no personal guarantee beyond A$0.5M**.
- **What this lens adds:** licence lead times, liability that insurance won't cover, who funds the capital and on what terms, and what the founder must put in. Stage-1 P(positive) figures are adjusted for those factors and nothing else.

## Candidate notes

**B0, stay employed.** No licence, no capital and no tail. Its cost is opportunity. Keeping the job while testing is valuable under this lens because it funds the regulatory set-up (TPB registration, legal opinions, accreditation) without forcing the founder to spend capital. Every candidate scored here assumes 6–18 months of staged testing, so B0 is part of every path for a while.

**B1, staged distribution holdco.** Licensing is light: a trading business needs no financial-services licence. Two problems, though. Acquisition debt for SME buy-outs usually needs **personal guarantees** from the sponsor (banks lend on cash flow plus the guarantor) [estimate], so it breaks the A$0.5M guarantee cap. Vendor finance lowers the cheque but leaves the founder carrying the seller's representations. Round 1 gave it P(positive) of about 32%. The brief has excluded it, and this lens finds no reason to bring it back.

**S1, Closewise.** Stage 1 understated three issues.
- **Registration is the critical path.** Providing BAS or tax services for a fee needs TPB registration. A company can register only if it has enough registered individuals to supervise the work. In practice that means the CA/tax-agent co-founder is a gating hire, not a later one.
- **Minimum PI cover is A$1M once turnover exceeds A$500k** [verified: TPB PI page]. The TPB sets no minimum run-off period; CPA Australia expects about 7 years [verified: TPB webinar; CPA via search]. Run-off is a cost the business keeps paying after it stops trading.
- **The headline promise, "ATO penalties on us", is the weak point.** Tax-audit policies expressly exclude tax, penalties, GIC and shortfall interest [verified: insurer product pages]. PI wordings commonly exclude liability assumed by contract unless it would have existed anyway [verified: CBP and policy wordings]. So a **contractual penalty indemnity is probably not covered by PI and has to be self-funded.** It needs a capped reserve (I'd hold 3–5% of compliance fees) and a cap at 1–3 months' fees. Service credits have the same problem.

TPB(GS) 55/2026 (final, 22 Jul 2026) confirms that practitioners remain responsible for AI output and need client consent before client data goes into third-party models [verified: TPB, Accountants Daily]. That makes model-vendor contracts and consent clauses day-1 tasks.

The **Exit-Ready** variant has its own issues. Deferred fees are unsecured business credit to weak companies, which brings adverse selection. Holding warrants in clients is fine for a non-auditor. But pooling warrants with outside LPs creates a managed investment scheme and needs an AFSL or authorised-representative (AR) arrangement. Warrants also trigger income tax on receipt. Capital is easy (no balance sheet), so the lens score is mid-table, held back by an uninsured guarantee and a floor of about 1x revenue.

**S2, Resources Finance Office (my top pick).** Its core licensing is the same as S1's (TPB plus PI). The extra layer is the **company-secretary role**. A public company's secretary is an "officer" [verified: secondary], so the individual is personally exposed to Corporations Act officer duties and civil penalties. The company can't indemnify an officer against some liabilities (s199A) [verified: Holding Redlich]. The fix is cheap but must be in the contract: the individual cosec has to be named on the client's D&O policy, with a deed of access, indemnity and insurance, and the firm needs its own management-liability cover. Without that, the founder's best people won't take the appointments.

Each asset option has a licensing issue stage 1 didn't flag:
- **(a) Royalty-for-fees.** The royalty is non-cash consideration, so the firm pays tax on its market value with no cash in hand [estimate]. In WA, an assignment of a royalty may not be dutiable under the Duties Act 2008, depending on its terms [verified: Mondaq commentary; older]. WA now charges duty on grants and transfers of **"derivative mining rights"** on the greater of consideration and market value, and treats them as land for landholder duty [verified: WA DMR fact sheet via search]. Whether a particular royalty deed falls inside that definition needs a ruling. WA royalties are generally not caveatable (round-1 source), so a royalty may not bind a later tenement holder.
- **(b) Runway Capital.** Refund advances and bridge notes are business-purpose credit, outside the National Credit Code and so needing no Australian Credit Licence [estimate: standard reading]. Pooling outside investors' money does need an AFSL or AR. ASIC is now at the enforcement stage on private credit, with REP 820 in Nov 2025, on-site surveillance and stop orders in 2026 [verified: Hall & Wilcox, Allens, Clayton Utz]. A first-time manager pays for that in compliance cost and slower LP onboarding. **Radium Capital already dominates R&D-refund lending**: more than A$1B advanced to over 1,000 companies, typically 85% of the refund, with plans to deploy A$400M [verified: FinTech Australia; Pilot Energy ASX Dec 2025]. So start by **referring** advances to Radium for a fee rather than lending.
- **(c) Closure deal flow.** This is only an option, not a business (see S8).

Funding is the best on the list: the business is self-funding from quarterly fees in advance, and the founder cheque is A$0.3–0.6M. P(total loss) is low. The floor is a book that a Perth firm would buy.

**S3, Contractor Cash Desk.**
- **Licensing.** B2B receivables finance needs no ACL [estimate]. Collecting debts *for clients* may need a state debt-collector or commercial-agent licence in some states (WA's Debt Collectors Licensing Act is still the one to check) [unverified]. Raising first-loss money from outside investors needs an AFSL or AR, as in S2.
- **Capital is the binding constraint.** A warehouse lends 85–90% but wants 10–15% first loss, so A$4–8M for a A$40–60M book. That money has to come from family offices or mezzanine funds. Warehouse providers usually want sponsor support or a guarantee on the first facility [estimate], which breaks the guarantee cap. Private-credit funding now faces ASIC scrutiny of credit-fund valuations [verified].
- **The approved-invoice thesis is narrower than stage 1 said.** Rio Tinto pays suppliers with turnover up to A$10M within **20 days**, and BHP signed the BCA supplier payment code (30 days for small businesses) [verified: Business Wire 2020, MiningNews, SmartCompany; dated]. The 60-day gap mainly hits contractors above about A$10M turnover. That cuts the target base, and those firms can usually get bank trade finance.
- **Tail risk.** Fraud and set-off can eat the first-loss layer, and a fraud loss is uninsured unless trade-credit cover is bought.

Run it off-balance-sheet first, as a referral business. Lend from its own balance sheet only with 24 months of loss data.

**S4, SPV Office.** This is the lightest licensing load among the services candidates.
- **Licensing.** SPV BAS and GST work needs TPB registration. There is no ASX officer role. Payments must be initiated from the SPV's own accounts under dual approval, so the firm **never holds client money** and stays outside payments licensing (Tranche 1 reforms, flagged by lens 12).
- **The real liability is third-party reliance.** Lenders rely on covenant and DSCR certificates and CIS settlement reports. If the firm signs reliance letters, a misstated DSCR becomes a negligent-misstatement claim from a bank, with no fee cap in the engagement contract to limit it. Rules: SPV directors sign the certificates, the firm gives no reliance letters, the cap is set at 12 months' fees, and PI cover is A$5–10M (about A$30–60k a year [estimate]).
- **Funding.** No balance sheet and the smallest founder cheque on the list (A$0.3–0.5M). Contracts run 10–15 years (the OX2 model) [verified by stage 1], so the floor is the best of the services candidates.
- **Track B bridge.** The bridge to underwriting needs aggregated **data rights in the contract from day 1**. Sponsors and lenders will push back. The bridge also needs no licence until the firm writes an actual floor or guarantee, which would need insurer capacity, as in S6.

Its slow sales cycle is the main reason it scores below S2.

**S5, ColdCore + MeterProof.**
- **MeterProof (VEU).** To create certificates it needs ESC accreditation as an Accredited Person, and project-based activities go through a separate PBA application [verified: ESC Application Guide v1.6, Nov 2025; PBA guide]. The ESC weighs conduct history heavily: it refused Astra Green's renewal in Sep 2026 [verified: Medianet]. A founder can't get accredited fast. The realistic route is **12–18 months under an existing Accredited Person's accreditation on a revenue share**. MeterProof needs little capital and no balance sheet, and taken alone it would score about **6.5**.
- **ColdCore has three capital and structure problems stage 1 missed.**
  1. **Off-balance-sheet appeal may be illusory.** A savings-share contract over identified equipment on the customer's site may be a lease under AASB 16, which puts it on the customer's balance sheet [estimate: needs accounting review]. That removes a key reason to buy.
  2. **Title risk.** Equipment bolted to a leased cold store can become a landlord's fixture. The owner needs PPSA registration plus landlord waivers on every site.
  3. **CEFC money arrives indirectly.** I found no CEFC equity commitment to a C&I energy-as-a-service platform in 2025–26. CEFC money reaches this market through bank green-loan programmes and funds such as Infradebt [verified: no hit; Infradebt via stage 1].
- **Funding terms.** The first 3–5 sites (A$1–2M each) need seed equity, and probably the founder's guarantee on early debt. After seed the founder is diluted to 35–50%, and to 20–30% by exit.

**S6, PassMark → VoltCover 2.0.** This is the most licence-heavy candidate.
- **ASIC's position.** Extended warranties are generally financial products. When a **third party** provides one, it is more likely to be insurance, and the incidental-product exemption generally doesn't apply [verified: ASIC extended-warranties page].
- **PassMark's defect guarantee.** It covers *installers'* defects, not just PassMark's own audit error. That is probably risk transfer, and carrying on insurance business without APRA authorisation is an offence [estimate: Insurance Act 1973]. The fix is to cap it as a service credit on PassMark's own fee, which guarantees only PassMark's audit, or to put it under an insurer from day 1.
- **VoltCover.** Homeowners are retail clients, so VoltCover needs an AFSL or AR status under the carrier, design-and-distribution obligations, AFCA membership and anti-hawking compliance.
- **Capacity.** The 2026 Australian market is soft, which helps capacity [verified: trade press]. But the MGAA warns of discipline, a new 10–15-year line with correlated OEM-default risk is the kind carriers decline, and Australia has no insurance-backed battery warranty precedent.
- **Value and timing.** Commission starts around year 2–3. Discounting at 8% a year hurts the MGA value, and the founder is diluted to about 35%. PassMark alone is fine, but the lens can't score the combination above 5.

**S7, Long Tail Royalties.**
- **Licensing.** If it raises equity into an operating company buying royalties, using the s708 wholesale exemptions, it can avoid an AFSL [estimate]. A fund with fees and carry is a managed investment scheme and needs an AFSL or AR.
- **Duty and title.** State duty and title are deal-by-deal costs. WA's derivative-mining-right rules and landholder duty need checking. WA royalties are not generally caveatable, so diligence on enforceability against later tenement holders is expensive.
- **Capital.** Capital is the problem: A$15–25M from family offices for a first-time resources manager. The founder ends with 15–25% after two raises and puts in A$0.5M.
- **Floor and timing.** The floor is genuinely strong: royalties carry no capex calls and a book sells at 0.6–1.0x cost. Cash yield is thin until the book is seeded with producing royalties, and most of the value arrives after year 6, so the time discount hurts it.

It works best as the **vehicle that later holds S2's royalties**.

**S8, Closure Co.**
- **Regulatory approval.** Transfers need regulator approval. In Qld that means a financial-provisioning assessment of the new holder, and surrender may need a **residual risk payment** to the state [verified: Qld business.qld.gov.au; FPS Act current at 1 Oct 2025].
- **Personal exposure.** Qld's chain-of-responsibility provisions (EPOLA 2016) let the regulator pursue **related persons, including directors**, of an under-resourced holder [verified: McCullough Robertson headline; detail estimate]. Ring-fencing in an SPV doesn't fully protect the founder personally. Contamination and perpetual water treatment are uninsurable tails.
- **Surety capacity is thin.** Assetinsure (with Swiss Re) was the first Australian insurer to write rehabilitation bonds. Amparo (Munich Re) focuses on construction surety [verified: Insurance Business, InsuranceNews]. New sureties for a first-time holder usually need cash collateral.
- **Precedents.** Real deals do happen: TerraCom bought Blair Athol for A$1 and received A$80M, Medallion took Forrestania's rehabilitation, and Austral took on Lady Loretta's [verified]. But the buyers are operators with a reprocessing or mining reason, not a stand-alone closure firm.

On this lens it's last.

## Promotions/demotions

1. **Merge S2 and S4 into one regulated chassis.** It would have one TPB-registered company and one PI programme (A$5–10M, run-off included). There would be two client books: ASX juniors and processors, and energy SPVs. That shares the hardest fixed costs (registration, the supervising CA, PI, the AI-governance policy under GS 55/2026, the D&O and officer-protection playbook). It also hedges the commodity cycle with 10–15-year SPV contracts. Sequence: start with whichever segment converts first.
2. **Promote MeterProof on its own and demote ColdCore to a later option.** The asset-light certificate engine, run under a partner Accredited Person, keeps the statistics edge without the AASB 16, fixture and guarantee problems. ColdCore can follow only if one CEFC-backed lender gives terms without a personal guarantee.
3. **Treat refund advances and approved-invoice finance (S2b, S3) as referral businesses first.** Earn origination fees from Radium-type lenders or bank SCF programmes until there are 24 months of loss data. Only then raise first loss under an AR arrangement.
4. **Promote "Offset Capital" (CMPTI claim engine, lens 03) as a module of S2, not a company.** The first claims arrive in FY28, and the registered-agent chassis is already in place for it.
5. **Demote S8 to a watch item.** Its 90-day test should be one meeting with the Qld regulator and one with a surety provider, nothing more.
6. **Restrict S6 to PassMark, with the guarantee capped to its own audit fee.** VoltCover should need a binder term sheet before any spending.

## Ranking and recommendation

1. **S2, Resources Finance Office (7.5).** It has the best mix of self-funding, a small founder cheque, licensing he can solve (TPB plus officer protection) and optional asset layers. Each layer can be switched on only after its own licence or partner is in place.
2. **S4, SPV Office (7.0).** It has the lightest uninsured tail once reliance letters are refused, the longest contracts and no balance sheet. Its sales cycle is slow.
3. **S1, Closewise (6.0).** Easy to fund, but the "penalties on us" promise is uninsured. Recast it as capped service credits and it becomes a sound but generic agency.

**My choice: S2 built on the merged S2+S4 regulated chassis.** The first contracts should promise capped service credits only, with no penalty indemnity and no lender reliance letters. Every capital product starts as a referral and moves onto the firm's own balance sheet only once data and an AR licence are in place.

## Sequencing

**While employed (months 0–9; get employer consent first):**
1. Recruit the registered tax-agent/CA co-founder. Agree terms for the company's TPB registration and line up a PI quote of A$5–10M aggregate, including run-off.
2. Commission one legal memo (about A$20–30k) covering:
   - the contractual-liability exclusion for capped service credits;
   - cosec D&O and deed terms;
   - whether a debt-collector licence is needed for AR work in WA, Qld and NSW;
   - WA duty on royalty-for-fee deeds;
   - the AFSL/AR route for any pooled vehicle.
3. Build the Appendix 5B data set and the SPV shadow close. Run 3 paid pilots, delivered by the co-founder under her registration.

**Quit gate.** Quit when there is ≥A$0.5M of contracted ARR across juniors and SPVs, PI is bound, and the cumulative founder cheque is ≤A$0.6M.

**Years 1–3.** Grow the chassis. Refer R&D and CMPTI advances to Radium-type lenders for fees. Collect royalty-for-fee consideration only from clients whose boards approve, inside a separate entity, with the tax on receipt funded from cash fees. Optionally run MeterProof as a side licence-partnered module if a Victorian cold-store opportunity appears through the Fulfilio network.

**Years 3–5.** With 8+ quarters of data, decide between two paths:
- (a) raise A$3–5M of first loss under an AR arrangement for refund advances or approved invoices;
- (b) seed S7 with S2's royalty book, raising from family offices as an operating company.

Leave insurance products (S6, SPV performance floors) until a carrier is on paper. Leave S8 out unless an operator partner carries the environmental authority.
